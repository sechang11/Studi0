# THE DERBY — Episode 1: "The Last Minute of the Year"

~20 minutes. Two characters, one match, one question.

**Protagonist: VIRO** (7) — playmaker. Gifted, warm, popular. Has never beaten RASK.
**Antagonist: RASK** (9) — striker. Cold, disciplined, unbeaten in this fixture.

The antagonist is not a villain. He is a wall. That distinction is what keeps the ending
from being cheap — the episode ends in a draw, not a victory, and the draw is earned.

**Dramatic question (posed 1:20, answered 19:10):** Can VIRO beat a man who has never lost
to him — and if he can't, is a draw worth anything?

**Planted / echoed / paid (rule of three):** a boy in the front row holding a hand-painted
number 7. Seen in the cold open (he leaves early). Echoed at the low point (his seat is
empty). Paid at the climax (he came back, and he is the only one standing).

---

## COLD OPEN — 0:00–1:30

Last year's derby, final whistle. Rain of confetti for the wrong team. VIRO on his knees on
the halfway line. RASK walks past him without looking down — not cruel, just *finished*.
In the stand a boy with a painted 7 is pulled away by his father before the whistle stops
ringing.

`establish` empty stand · `master` the whistle · `react` VIRO on knees · `insert` boy's
sign left on the seat · `pillow` confetti settling on wet grass

> **VIRO** (not to anyone) "…Again."

**Withheld:** RASK's face. We see his back, his boots, his shadow. Not his eyes.

## TITLE — 1:30–2:00

Card: **THE DERBY** — over the empty stadium at dawn.

---

## ACT ONE — 2:00–7:00 · *what he wants*

**Scene 1 — Tunnel, present day (2:00).** One year later. Same fixture. VIRO taping his
wrist. Teammates loud around him; he is not.

**Scene 2 — Locker room (3:10).** The manager's team talk. The information the audience
needs, delivered as pressure rather than exposition: last game of the season, VIRO's last
derby before his transfer, and the club has never beaten them with him in the side.

> **MANAGER** "Nine years. Nine years we've come here and gone home quiet."
> **VIRO** "I know what the record is."
> **MANAGER** "Then stop trying to beat *him*. Beat the team."

That line is the episode's thesis and VIRO ignores it until 15:00.

**Scene 3 — RASK introduced properly (5:00).** Opposite tunnel. He is alone, taping his own
ankle, no entourage. **First clear look at his face.** He is not sneering. He is *bored* —
which is worse, and tells you everything about what VIRO is up against.

> **RASK** (to a teammate, flat) "He'll come at me early. He always does."

**Scene 4 — Walk-out (6:20).** The two lines converge in the tunnel mouth. They do not
speak. The boy with the painted 7 is back in the front row.

`establish` · `master` · `speak` ×6 · `react` ×5 · `insert` taped wrist, painted sign ·
`pillow` floodlights warming up

**By 7:00 the audience can state:** VIRO wants to beat RASK personally. That want is the
flaw, and the manager already named it.

## EYECATCH — 7:00–7:15

---

## ACT TWO — 7:15–15:00 · *what stops him*

**Scene 5 — Kick-off, first exchange (7:15).** VIRO goes straight at RASK, exactly as
predicted. Gets nothing. Shot to establish the geography of the match.

**Scene 6 — RASK scores (9:00).** Against the run of play, almost casually. **1–0.** The
goal is deliberately *undramatic* — no sakuga here. Saving it is the whole point.

`build` · `insert` ball crossing the line · `react` VIRO, from distance, small in frame

**Scene 7 — The long middle (10:00).** VIRO tries and fails. Three attempts, each shorter
on screen than the last — the edit itself running out of patience with him.

**Scene 8 — The setback (12:00).** VIRO is booked for a rash challenge on RASK. One more and
he is off. He has made himself the story, and it has cost him the ability to compete.

> **RASK** (helping him up, genuinely) "You're playing me. Play the game."

The antagonist gives the protagonist the same advice as his own manager. This is the beat
the episode is built around.

**Scene 9 — Low point (13:30).** VIRO alone at the edge of the box. Crowd noise drops out.
`hold_silent`. **The boy's seat is empty.**

`pillow` empty seat · `react` VIRO seeing it · `pillow` clock reading 88:00

**By 15:00:** he has lost the thing he was playing for, and been told twice how to fix it.

## ACT THREE — 15:00–19:00 · *the change*

**Scene 10 — The decision (15:00).** VIRO stops. Lets a ball go past him. Something settles
in his face. He stops chasing RASK.

**Scene 11 — The last minute (15:40).** He plays the *team*. Quick exchanges, one touch,
his head up. RASK reads him and finds nothing there to read.

**Scene 12 — SAKUGA (17:00).** The one burst. Ninety seconds of everything the episode has
withheld: speed, impact, colour, the full effects vocabulary of the vertical short — which
now lands *because* nineteen minutes of restraint preceded it.

**Scene 13 — Silence (18:30).** `hold_silent`. VIRO and the ball. No score. No crowd.

**Scene 14 — The equaliser (18:50).** **1–1.**

`sakuga` · `insert` net · `react` RASK, finally something in his face

## TAG — 19:00–20:00

Final whistle. A draw. Nobody celebrates like it's a win, because it isn't one.

RASK offers his hand. VIRO takes it.

> **RASK** "You still haven't beaten me."
> **VIRO** "I know." *(beat)* "But you didn't beat me either."

Wide: the boy with the painted 7, back in his seat, the only person in the stand standing.

> **RASK** (walking away, half-turned) "…Next year, then."

VIRO is transferring. There is no next year. He doesn't correct him.

`pillow` painted 7 on an empty seat · slow crane out · cut to black

---

## Why this should hold 20 minutes

- **The antagonist is right.** RASK and the manager give identical advice. VIRO's obstacle
  is internal, so the runtime is spent on a person rather than on a scoreline.
- **The climax is earned by restraint.** No sakuga before 17:00. RASK's goal is deliberately
  flat so the equaliser has somewhere to go.
- **The ending withholds the win.** A draw, and the audience is told there is no next year
  while VIRO chooses not to say so. That is the last thing withheld.
- **Two speaking parts.** Cheap to voice, and forces the drama into faces and silence,
  which is what the format is good at.
